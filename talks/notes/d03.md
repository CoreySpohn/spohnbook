## Lesson

Every radiometric symbol is a density over area, wavelength, solid angle or
time, and one pixel's expected electron count is the integral of their product
over the sky cell, the band and the live interval, with QE inside the
wavelength integral.

## What to say

1. Still 1. Light travels to the right. The star is a point source; its photon
   flux density at the aperture is per square meter, per nanometer, per second.
   The green patch is extended; its brightness is also per steradian. The
   dashed cell is the patch of sky an idealized mapping assigns to pixel $p$,
   and it subtends $\Omega_p$ at the aperture. The aperture sets the area $A$,
   the optics transmit $T_{\rm opt}$, and the detector converts photons to
   electrons with QE $q_p$. The image is not a point: the profile beside the
   pixel column shows the light spreading, and $P_p(\lambda)$ is the share
   landing in pixel $p$; it changes with wavelength because the image grows.
   At right, the photon rate arriving in $p$ from the point source is still a
   density per nanometer. For the extended source, the integral of
   $I_\lambda P_p$ over solid angle takes the place of $\Phi_\lambda P_p$. The
   electron count is what remains after the wavelength and time integrals.
   The tags under each element say which single factor holds each loss.
2. Still 1a. The side view again, one row per kind of source. Top row: the
   factors of the point-source term sit where they act. The source sets
   $\Phi_\lambda$, per square meter, per nanometer, per second. The aperture
   sets $A$. The optics transmit $T_{\rm opt}$. The image spreads over the
   pixel column, and $P_p(\lambda,\boldsymbol\theta)$ is the share in pixel $p$ of a
   point source at direction $\boldsymbol\theta$, the heavily shaded part of the
   profile. The detector QE $q_p$ turns those photons into electrons. The
   title multiplies them in that order. Bottom row: an extended source has a
   brightness $I_\lambda$, also per steradian. Straight lines through the lens
   center carry the dashed sky cell onto pixel $p$, and the image is
   inverted. The shaded cone, with its vertex at the lens center, is the
   cell's solid angle $\Omega_p$. That cell is what the chapter integrates over when it writes the
   pixel's flux as the integral of $I_\lambda$ over $\Omega_p$. Now take a
   direction one pixel pitch from the cell center, half a pitch beyond its
   edge. Its image is centered on the
   neighboring pixel, but the image spreads, and its tail reaches into $p$.
   Light from inside the cell spreads out of $p$ in the same way. So the pixel
   receives the integral of $I_\lambda P_p$ over all directions, and that
   takes the place of $\Phi_\lambda P_p$. At right, the same pixel face-on.
   It returns on the next slide.
3. Still 2. The same pixel face-on, at one wavelength: a point source spreads
   over neighboring pixels, so a pixel never receives one ray or one sky cell.
   Beside it, the spectrum, the optical transmission, the pixel's share $P_p$
   and the QE all change with wavelength; the curves are normalized shapes.
   They are multiplied at each wavelength first, and only then is the band
   integrated. Applying a band-center QE to an integrated rate gives a
   different answer whenever QE varies across the band.
4. Still 3. The band integral from the left panel is the electron rate, and
   the right panel draws it as the slope of the expected count. The count
   grows only while the pixel is live, meaning exposing rather than being read
   out, over $t_{\rm live}$, and stops there, reaching $\mu_{e,p}$. This is an expectation: a real frame scatters
   about it and adds detector noise, which the detector chapter treats.
5. Worked example. The reference experiment makes every factor trivial: a
   rectangular 1 nm spectrum with the photon density $\Phi_{\lambda,\mathrm{nm}}$
   of 1 Jy at 700 nm, 21,559.86 photon per second per square meter per
   nanometer. Times 1 square meter and 1 nm gives 21,559.86 photon per second;
   times 1 second over four pixels gives 5,389.96 photons each, an equal split
   by construction. With constant QE $q$ and no detector noise, the summed
   electron count, and numerically its Poisson variance in electron squared,
   is $q$ times 21,559.86. It is a code-verification fixture against an
   analytic anchor, not validation.

## Assumptions

- Stills 1 to 3 and 1a: the chapter's expected-count integral for a point source,
  with the extended-source angular integral shown through an idealized pixel
  footprint. Rates are constant over the live interval. Curves show shapes
  only, and the Airy image is illustrative, for an unobscured circular
  aperture at one lambda/D per pixel.
- Still 1a: the rays through the lens center are chief rays, which keep
  their angle, so the drawn cell maps exactly onto pixel $p$ and the drawn
  outside direction, one pixel pitch from the cell center, lands on the next pixel.
  The profiles are cuts through the same illustrative Airy pattern as the
  face-on panel, before pixel integration, so their shaded areas show where
  light falls, not the value of $P_p$. The cell is an idealized footprint.
  If asked whether the cell integral is then wrong: for a uniform field, a
  spread that does not change across the field and no light lost, what the
  spread carries out of the cell equals what it brings in, so the two
  integrals agree; they differ where the field has structure, such as a
  bright neighbor.
- The ownership tags (area in $A$, optical losses in $T_{\rm opt}$, attenuation
  and finite capture in $P_p$, QE once in $q_p$) follow the chapter's proposed
  ledger; they are a proposal, not an adopted interface.
- The worked example is a code-verification fixture. It covers no real
  spectrum, optical loss, coronagraph, dark current, clock-induced charge,
  read noise or stochastic readout. The four-pixel split is by construction,
  not a point-spread function.
