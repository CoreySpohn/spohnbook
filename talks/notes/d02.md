## Lesson

The illumination angle is measured at the planet and the observer-axis angle at the star, and an orbit's three angles are fixed by a declared observer direction, a north/east basis and a node rule, not by the picture on the sky.

## What to say

1. Construction still. The star is at the origin and the observer is far to the right. Starlight travels along r to the planet. Drop the planet onto the line of sight to get Z, and onto the sky plane to get the projected separation rho. The observer-axis angle is measured at the star from the observer direction; the illumination angle is measured at the planet, between the way back to the star and the way to the observer. For a distant observer the two add to pi.
2. Three-rotations clip (or its still). The chapter's formula is three rotations, and read left to right each one turns about an axis the one before it placed. First the whole ellipse turns about the line of sight by Omega, 130 deg from north toward east; the line it carries along becomes the node line. The camera then swings to an oblique view. Second, the orbit tilts by i, 55 deg, about that node line; the normal h leaves the line of sight, and the node where the planet now moves toward us is the ascending node. Third, the orbit turns by omega_p, 70 deg, about h, inside its own plane, and periapsis leaves the node. What is left on screen is the orbit-elements picture.
3. Orbit-elements still. The basis is north, east, toward the observer, and it is right-handed. The ascending node is where the planet crosses the sky plane moving toward us. Omega runs in the sky plane from north toward east to that node. The inclination is the angle from the observer direction to the angular momentum. The argument of periapsis runs in the orbit plane from the node to periapsis, in the direction of motion. On the right is what the observer sees: north up, east to the left, Omega counterclockwise on the image from north to the ascending node, and offsets reported as east then north. The oblique camera is turned 15 deg from east toward the observer and raised 15 deg toward north, so east and the observer direction do not fall on one line.
4. Viewpoint sweep. Nothing in space moves; only the camera turns about north, from the side view to the observer's own view. The orbit on the sky is a projection of one three-dimensional state. The page scale is fixed, so the change of shape is foreshortening, not a change of orbit.
5. Orbit clock. Now the cameras are fixed and time runs at equal steps. Watch the side view: behind the sky plane the planet is nearly full, at the sky plane it is half lit, in front it is a crescent. Quadrature happens exactly where the planet crosses the sky plane, because cos alpha equals minus Z over r.

For the sweep, the one variable is the camera angle about north; for the clock, it is orbital time. In the three-rotations clip one angle changes at a time, and the camera turns only between the first and second rotations, never during one. Every scale is fixed across frames in all three.

## Assumptions

- Point-source star, distant observer, two-body Keplerian relative orbit, Lambert-sphere planet.
- The observer basis, the node rule and the east/north output order are the chapter's proposed profile, pending at the conventions stage. The east direction of the second axis is the chapter's reading; one sentence of Savransky et al. (2019) calls that axis west, and the three-axis basis transform fixture must settle it.
- The drawings are computed from the chapter's formulas. The current orbix projection applies the same rotation but labels its first rotated component RA and its second Dec, so for the same elements its sky plot is this one mirrored about the north-east diagonal: the node appears at position angle 90 deg minus Omega and the apparent motion is reversed. A reflection is not a rotation, so relabeling cannot migrate fitted elements.
- The orbit is drawn to scale; the observer distance is not, and the planet disk in the observer's view is enlarged. Nothing here is a measured system or a performance claim.
