## Lesson

Local zodiacal light and exozodiacal light are the same process, starlight scattered once by grains, but the telescope sits inside one cloud and far outside the other, so the two are described by different model inputs.

## What to say

1. Left: the Sun, the Solar-system dust around us, and the telescope inside it on the Earth orbit, seen from above the ecliptic. We look out along a half-ray and add up the light from every grain along it. The table we use needs only the look direction's ecliptic longitude from the Sun, its height above the ecliptic, our distance from the Sun and the wavelength. The arc shows the longitude difference, 135 degrees; because the sightline also rises 30 degrees, its true angle from the Sun, the solar elongation, is 128 degrees.
2. At one grain, sunlight arrives along the yellow ray and part of it is scattered back along the green ray to the telescope. The scattering angle is measured from the incoming direction to the direction toward the telescope: zero is forward. For this grain, 1 AU out along the sightline, it is 154 degrees, nearly backscattering. The illumination angle is its supplement, 26 degrees.
3. Right: a different star, about 10 parsecs away, with its own disk. We are far outside it; the break on the sightline stands for those 10 parsecs. Side on, the disk is tilted by its inclination; the half toward us is the near half, and a grain there scatters forward, at 30 degrees for this 60 degree disk. The highlighted stretch of the sightline is its path through the dust layer. The disk model takes its density, phase function, orientation (inclination and position angle), the star's luminosity and its distance.
4. Animation: the disk does not move; only our viewing direction turns, as the small disk-frame sketch shows. Two things change. The path through the thin dust layer gets longer, which brightens the whole ring. And the near-side grain moves toward forward scattering, where its phase function rises from 0.054 to 0.29 per steradian, while its far-side mirror moves toward backscattering, where it falls from 0.054 to 0.025, so the phase effect brightens the near half and dims the far half. The color scale is fixed across every frame.

## Assumptions

- Optically thin, single scattering of a point star; no thermal emission, extinction, multiple scattering or polarization.
- The ray, the scattering angle and the illumination angle are physical identities under those assumptions. The near-side and inclination profile is proposed; the observer basis and the sense of inclinations above 90 degrees are pending, so the image axes are named along the line of nodes and the projected minor axis, not east and north.
- The top view draws the sightline at a longitude difference of minus 135 degrees from the Sun; the Leinert table is symmetric in that sign.
- The local cloud outline and the disk layer thickness are schematic. The Solar-system panel shows no density model: the Leinert table gives brightness along one sightline from inside only.
- The disk image is a synthetic GRaTeR disk rendered by the skyscapes GraterDisk example implementation, divided by its own peak (still) or by the brightest frame (animation). It is not a validated disk model or a performance claim.
